# Philosophy

## Computation is Material

We often think of computation as a purely mathematical or abstract process. When we type `math.sin(x)`, we expect a result instantly. But for most of human history, computing `sin(x)` meant something very different. It meant physically manipulating tools—whether they were counting rods, beads on a wire, the gears of a brass machine, or simply ink on paper. 

Every numerical result had a **material cost**.

**DE RERUM COMPUTABILIUM** exists to surface this hidden cost.

## The Two Clocks

When looking at historical mathematics, we can fall into two traps:
1. **Presentism**: Judging an ancient method purely by how "inefficient" it is compared to a modern CPU.
2. **Romanticism**: Treating an ancient method as a magical lost art without understanding its profound, often grueling, operational reality.

To avoid this, we operate with **two clocks**. 
We look at the historical procedure on its own terms—how many additions did it take? What tables were available? How was rounding handled before floating-point standards?
Then, we compare it against a modern reference implementation. Not to judge the past, but to understand exactly how much labor we have compressed into our modern standard libraries.

## The Human Computer

Before a computer was a machine, it was a job title. 
Astronomers, actuarial clerks, ballistics teams, and surveying offices employed thousands of "human computers." The algorithms they used were specifically shaped by human constraints:
- Multiplication is hard; addition is easy. (Hence, logarithms and prosthaphaeresis).
- Memory is finite; paper is cheap but searching it is slow. (Hence, the physical layout of tables).
- Humans make errors. (Hence, differencing techniques and independent verification teams).

By writing code that executes these historical workflows, we pay homage to the humans who built the numerical foundations of the modern world.
